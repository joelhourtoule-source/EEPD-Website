RAMI and FMECA: building a maintenance strategy

How to go from RAMI and FMECA analyses to a maintenance plan and a spare parts policy based on criticality.

By Joël Hourtoule · 4 November 2026

From reliability analysis to maintenance plan: how equipment criticality sets maintenance and spare parts stock.

An electrical network is judged on its availability. It depends on the design, but also on how the installation is operated and maintained. RAMI and FMECA analyses focus maintenance effort on the equipment whose failure weighs the most, instead of treating all equipment the same way.

RAMI and FMECA: two complementary views

  • RAMI analysis (reliability, availability, maintainability…) assesses the architecture as a whole: what availability to expect from the distribution, and which equipment or redundancies limit it
  • FMECA (failure modes, effects and criticality analysis) examines each piece of equipment: how it can fail, with what consequences, and how it is detected

The steps of a FMECA

  • Describe the functions of the equipment and its environment
  • List the possible failure modes
  • Describe their causes and their effects on the system
  • Describe the existing means of detection
  • Assess criticality and rank the equipment
  • Define the actions: maintenance, redundancy, monitoring, spare parts

From criticality to maintenance policy

The criticality ranking is the starting point. For highly critical equipment, preventive maintenance and periodic tests are preferred. For equipment of medium criticality, condition-based maintenance, triggered by the measured state of the equipment, is often suitable. For low-criticality equipment, corrective maintenance may be accepted.

Spare parts and obsolescence

Criticality also guides stocks: parts for critical equipment are kept on site, stock is targeted for equipment of medium criticality, and the others are ordered on demand. Obsolescence calls for particular monitoring: equipment whose part is no longer manufactured can become a weak point. A standardisation analysis, carried out over several years, helps reduce the number of references to manage.

Indicators to follow

To steer the strategy, a few simple indicators are followed, defined from the start: availability of critical equipment, duration of outages, share of preventive maintenance completed on time, lead times for critical parts. The aim is not to multiply indicators, but to choose those that trigger a decision. Target values depend on the installation and its requirements: they are set with the operator.

Common mistakes

  • Doing the FMECA as a compliance document, without linking it to the maintenance plan
  • Treating all equipment with the same level of effort
  • Not feeding the analysis with operating feedback
  • Building a parts stock without considering obsolescence

A continuous improvement cycle

The analyses are not frozen. The history of failures and interventions, recorded in the maintenance management tool, makes it possible to correct the FMECA assumptions, adjust maintenance frequencies and revise the spare parts list.

Frequently asked questions

Should a FMECA be redone for an existing installation?

Yes, it is possible and useful: we start from the operating history to rank the equipment and review the maintenance plan.

When should the RAMI analysis be done?

Ideally during design, because it can change the architecture, for example by adding redundancy.

How many spare parts should be stocked?

It depends on criticality, lead times and the targeted availability: the FMECA provides the elements to decide, equipment by equipment.
